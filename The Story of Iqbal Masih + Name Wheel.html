Strategy 8 · Annotating text
Read together · PART 1 · CHILDREN AT WORK
¶1Each morning, six days a week, more than half a million children ages four to fourteen rise before dawn and walk along dark roads to Pakistan's carpet factories. Most must be at work by 6:00 AM. If they are late, they may be hit with a wooden cane — or worse. The weavers work 14 hours a day, with only a 30-minute lunch break.
¶2Iqbal Masih was one of these workers. He started working in a carpet factory when he was just four years old. His parents were poor farmers near Lahore, the largest city in Pakistan. They had no money for food or clothes, so for about $16 they sent their son to work until he could pay back the loan. Iqbal was told he would earn three cents a day.
¶3A man named Arshad owned the factory. The only light came from two bare light bulbs that hung like a dragon's eyes. The carpet looms looked a hundred years old. Two strong wooden beams ran across the top and bottom of each loom's frame, held up by four large stakes driven into the ground.
Q · answer with evidence: Why did Iqbal have to work in the carpet factory?
LEVEL 2I think ___ because ¶__ says ___.
LEVEL 4According to ¶__, "___" — this shows ___.
Model answer + rationale

Why did Iqbal have to work in the carpet factory?

Answer: To help pay back his parents' loan.

Evidence: ¶2 says his parents took about $16 and sent him to work “until he could pay back the loan.” He was told he would earn three cents a day.

Read together · PART 1 · INSIDE THE FACTORY
¶4In front of each loom sat a small child on a wooden scaffold. The young weavers tied short pieces of brightly colored thread to a warp of heavier white threads. To make one carpet, workers tied more than a million small knots into intricate designs of circles and squares. In the United States, carpets like these sell for more than $2,000 each.
¶5The children never spoke to one another, except for a rare, forbidden whisper. “If I let them talk, I know they will start making mistakes,” Iqbal's boss said. “And when they make mistakes, I lose money.” If the children complained about how they were treated, they were beaten. Over the years, Iqbal received many cuts and bruises from Arshad's punishments.
¶6One night, when Iqbal was ten years old, Arshad pulled him out of bed at 3:00 AM and ordered him to repair some carpets. Iqbal went to the local police to complain. He told them that his boss had beaten him, and he showed them the bruises on his arms.
Q · answer with evidence: Why were the children not allowed to speak to each other?
LEVEL 2I think ___ because ¶__ says ___.
LEVEL 4According to ¶__, "___" — this shows ___.
Model answer + rationale

Why were the children not allowed to speak to each other?

Answer: The boss believed talking would cause mistakes — and mistakes cost him money.

Evidence: ¶5 gives his exact words: “If I let them talk, I know they will start making mistakes… when they make mistakes, I lose money.”

Read together · PART 1 · NO HELP FROM THE POLICE
¶7One police officer glared at Iqbal. He said Iqbal had no right to complain — he had better stick to his work and do what he was told. The officer grabbed Iqbal by his sore arm and led him back to the factory. “If he tries this again, chain him to his loom,” the officer told Arshad.
¶8Arshad did chain Iqbal to his loom. Even when Iqbal hurt so much he could hardly move, he fought back. He believed that what Arshad was doing was wrong.
¶9At ten years old, Iqbal was just under four feet tall — the size of a child two or three years younger. He weighed less than 60 pounds. From years of sitting hunched in front of the loom, his spine curved like an old man's, and his feet shuffled slowly when he walked.
Q · answer with evidence: Why do you think the police officer did NOT help Iqbal? (inference)
LEVEL 2I think ___ because ¶__ says ___.
LEVEL 4According to ¶__, "___" — this shows ___.
Model answer + rationale

Why do you think the police officer did NOT help Iqbal? (inference)

Answer: Sample: the police protected the factory owners, not the children.

Evidence: Evidence: ¶7 the officer grabbed Iqbal's sore arm and said “chain him to his loom.” ¶17 adds: police did not enforce the labor law.

Read together · PART 2 · THE DEBT GROWS — AND A MEETING
¶10Arshad told Iqbal that the harder he worked, the faster the loan would be paid off. But no matter what Iqbal did, the loan just got bigger. His father left home, and his mother had to borrow more money from Arshad. By the time Iqbal was eleven, the loan had grown to $419 — more than 25 times the original amount. Iqbal knew he would be trapped forever unless he escaped.
¶11In the summer of 1992, Iqbal heard about a meeting in a nearby town. A man named Essan Ulla Khan was going to speak about a new law that forbade carpet factories to employ children. Iqbal decided he must go. On the day of the meeting, he had already worked almost ten hours in 120-degree heat. Exhausted, he pushed through the crowd and sat on the floor below the platform where Khan was speaking.
¶12Khan talked about an organization called the Bonded Labor Liberation Front (BLLF). Its goal was to free Pakistan's bonded laborers — workers treated like slaves, owned like property. Thousands of children worked in bondage in carpet, brick, and leather factories. Under the new law, bonded laborers did not have to work if they did not want to.
Q · answer with evidence: Why did Iqbal's loan keep getting bigger, even though he worked hard?
LEVEL 2I think ___ because ¶__ says ___.
LEVEL 4According to ¶__, "___" — this shows ___.
Model answer + rationale

Why did Iqbal's loan keep getting bigger, even though he worked hard?

Answer: The debt was a trap — interest grew, and his mother had to borrow even more.

Evidence: ¶10: “no matter what Iqbal did, the loan just got bigger”; his mother borrowed more from Arshad. By age 11 it was $419 — 25 times the original $16.

Read together · PART 2 · NEW RIGHTS
¶13When Khan finished speaking, many people jumped up with questions. Finally Khan noticed Iqbal's small raised hand. After a pause, Iqbal asked quietly, “How can I stop working and go to school?” Khan explained that Iqbal had new rights under the law. He could show Arshad some legal papers, and Arshad would have to let him go. Khan also told him about BLLF schools for former bonded laborers.
¶14The next morning, Iqbal returned to the factory with the legal papers. He told Arshad he would no longer work, nor pay his debt, because bonded labor was illegal. Arshad's face grew red with anger. He cursed at Iqbal and beat him. But Iqbal escaped and ran out of the factory. Two days later, Arshad came to Iqbal's home, demanding that he return or pay. Iqbal stood his ground — he knew he could count on help from his new friend.
¶15Khan did help Iqbal get away from the factory. He threatened to have Arshad arrested if he protested. Khan greatly admired Iqbal's courage and perseverance, and he found Iqbal a place in a BLLF primary school in Lahore.
Q · answer with evidence: What did Khan tell Iqbal to do so he could stop working?
LEVEL 2I think ___ because ¶__ says ___.
LEVEL 4According to ¶__, "___" — this shows ___.
Model answer + rationale

What did Khan tell Iqbal to do so he could stop working?

Answer: Show Arshad the legal papers — bonded labor was now illegal, so Arshad had to let him go.

Evidence: ¶13: Iqbal asked “How can I stop working and go to school?” Khan said he had new rights under the law and told him about BLLF schools.

Read together · PART 3 · SCHOOL, THEN FIGHTING BACK
¶16Iqbal told his teachers he wanted to become a lawyer and fight for children's rights. He did not want any child to suffer the way he had. Some kids teased him, calling him “Chief Justice,” but he didn't care. He worked hard and was a good student. Every night after school, he brought a book to bed and read late into the night.
¶17Other children were not as lucky. Many did not hear about the new law. Factory owners kept workers from talking to the BLLF. The police did not enforce the labor law, and factories just ignored it.
¶18Iqbal and Khan started traveling together to talk about the new law and to free young bonded laborers. One day Khan took Iqbal to a carpet factory in a village called Kasur. Because Iqbal was so small, the guards let him in, thinking he was just another worker. Once inside, Iqbal asked the children questions: How often were they beaten? How often did they work overtime? How were they treated?
Q · answer with evidence: What did Iqbal do inside the carpet factory in Kasur, and why?
LEVEL 2I think ___ because ¶__ says ___.
LEVEL 4According to ¶__, "___" — this shows ___.
Model answer + rationale

What did Iqbal do inside the carpet factory in Kasur, and why?

Answer: He secretly interviewed the child workers to gather evidence.

Evidence: ¶18: because he was small, the guards let him in; inside he asked how often they were beaten and forced to work overtime. ¶19: Khan used the information in a report — police freed 300 children.

Read together · PART 3 · SPEAKING TO THE WORLD
¶19Khan used the information Iqbal gathered to write an investigative report. Because of the report, police raided the factory and found 300 children who had been tortured and beaten. They were all between the ages of four and ten.
¶20When Iqbal was twelve, he began speaking to huge crowds to demand better working conditions. People in Europe and the United States invited him to speak. He told audiences that the colorful carpets in their homes were made by children who lived as slaves. In the United States, ABC News named him “Person of the Week,” and the Reebok Corporation honored him with an award.
¶21When people learned how their carpets were made, they did not want to buy them anymore. In 1992, factories in Pakistan sold fewer carpets to foreign countries. At first the decline was slight, but two years later sales fell sharply.
Q · answer with evidence: Why did carpet sales to foreign countries fall after Iqbal spoke?
LEVEL 2I think ___ because ¶__ says ___.
LEVEL 4According to ¶__, "___" — this shows ___.
Model answer + rationale

Why did carpet sales to foreign countries fall after Iqbal spoke?

Answer: People stopped buying carpets once they learned children made them as slaves.

Evidence: ¶20: Iqbal told audiences their carpets “were made by children who lived as slaves.” ¶21: “When people learned how their carpets were made, they did not want to buy them anymore.”

Read together · PART 4 · THREATS — AND A DEATH
¶22Carpet factory owners and managers were furious. They blamed “subversive organizations” and “the child revolutionary.” Threats were made on Iqbal's life.
¶23On Easter Sunday in 1995, Iqbal went to visit relatives in a rural village. After spending time with his aunt, he and two cousins rode their bicycles to see his uncle, who was working in a nearby field. As the boys bounced along the dirt path, someone suddenly fired a shotgun at them from a short distance. Iqbal was killed instantly. He was twelve years old.
¶24No one knows exactly what happened or who killed Iqbal. Some people say it was an accident. Others say it was a murder arranged by the carpet manufacturers. The real facts may never be known. Many human rights groups accused the police of failing to investigate the crime thoroughly.
Q · answer with evidence: How did Iqbal die, and why do people suspect the carpet industry?
LEVEL 2I think ___ because ¶__ says ___.
LEVEL 4According to ¶__, "___" — this shows ___.
Model answer + rationale

How did Iqbal die, and why do people suspect the carpet industry?

Answer: He was shot while riding his bicycle; factory owners had threatened his life.

Evidence: ¶22: owners were furious and “threats were made on Iqbal's life.” ¶23: a shotgun was fired at the boys and Iqbal was killed instantly. ¶24: many believe it was arranged murder.

Read together · PART 4 · A VOICE THAT REMAINS
¶25At Iqbal's funeral, 800 mourners crowded into the small village cemetery. A week later, 3,000 protesters — half of them under the age of twelve — marched through the streets of Lahore.
¶26For many children working under harsh conditions, Iqbal Masih provided a voice. He gave them the courage to follow him out of bondage. His story brought attention to the plight of the world's working children.
Q · answer with evidence: What happened after Iqbal's death — and what does it show about his impact?
LEVEL 2I think ___ because ¶__ says ___.

Model answer + rationale

What happened after Iqbal's death — and what does it show about his impact?

Answer: Thousands mourned and marched; his story gave working children a voice and courage.

Evidence: ¶25: 800 mourners at his funeral, then 3,000 protesters marched in Lahore. ¶26: he “brought attention to the plight of the world's working children.”
