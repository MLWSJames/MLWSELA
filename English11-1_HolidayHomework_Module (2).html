General Rules

  • No 1st person (I, me, my opinion, my ideas)
  • No contractions (didn’t, wouldn’t, isn’t – write it out in full: did not, would not, is not)
  • Use formal language!
  • Use transition words to start each of your body paragraphs and concluding sentences.
  • Use ONLY the texts/evidence PROVIDED to you!

Structure Formula (for each of the 3 body paragraphs)

  1. Transition + Point.
  2. Reporting phrase + evidence + MLA citation.
  3. Explanation.
  4. Transition + concluding sentence.

The 5-Paragraph Essay Map

H L T P E E C P E E C P E E C T S C

1) The Introduction

  • H: Hook / Attention Getter
  • L: Lead-in statement / Thematic connection
  • T: Thesis Statement

2) Body Paragraph 1

  • P: Point / Topic Sentence
  • E: Evidence (Source 1)
  • E: Explanation (Source 1)
  • C: Concluding statement for the paragraph

3) Body Paragraph 2

  • P: Point / Topic Sentence
  • E: Evidence (Source 2)
  • E: Explanation (Source 2)
  • C: Concluding statement for the paragraph

4) Body Paragraph 3

  • P: Point / Topic Sentence
  • E: Evidence (Source 3)
  • E: Explanation (Source 3)
  • C: Concluding statement for the paragraph

5) Conclusion

  • T: Transition
  • S: Summary
  • C: Clincher / Final Thought

Introduction Paragraph — What Each Part Does

Hook: Gets the reader’s attention and makes them want to read more. It is a general statement.
Thematic statement / Lead-in: Shows the main topic (taken from the prompt).
Thesis statement: Argument and three points that will be discussed in the body paragraphs that will follow.

Body Paragraphs 1–3 — What Each Part Does

Body Paragraph #1

Transition: Tell the reader you are starting your first idea.
Point / Topic Statement: Use the first idea from your thesis statement and make a sentence to say what you are about to prove.
Reporting Phrase: Word or phrase to introduce your evidence, as well as providing the title of the text/evidence.
Evidence: Use quotes, summarized, or paraphrased information from Source 1 to support the point you are making. Remember: you need to correctly use MLA in-text citations.
Explanation: Explain how this evidence proves your topic statement and how it is linked to the prompt.
Concluding Statement: Sum up what you have discussed in the paragraph and restate your topic sentence in different words.

Body Paragraph #2

Transition: Tell the reader you are starting your second idea.
Point / Topic Statement: Use the second idea from your thesis statement and make a sentence to say what you are about to prove.
Reporting Phrase: Word or phrase to introduce your evidence, as well as providing the title of the text/evidence.
Evidence: Use quotes, summarized, or paraphrased information from Source 2 to support the point you are making. Remember: you need to correctly use MLA in-text citations.
Explanation: Explain how this evidence proves your topic statement and how it is linked to the prompt.
Concluding Statement: Sum up what you have discussed in the paragraph and restate your topic sentence in different words.

Body Paragraph #3

Transition: Tell the reader you are starting your third idea.
Point / Topic Statement: Use the third idea from your thesis statement and make a sentence to say what you are about to prove.
Reporting Phrase: Word or phrase to introduce your evidence, as well as providing the title of the text/evidence.
Evidence: Use quotes, summarized, or paraphrased information from Source 3 to support the point you are making. Remember: you need to correctly use MLA in-text citations.
Explanation: Explain how this evidence proves your topic statement and how it is linked to the prompt.
Concluding Statement: Sum up what you have discussed in the paragraph and restate your topic sentence in different words.

Conclusion Paragraph — What Each Part Does

Transition word: Begins the last paragraph (preferably one that sounds academic and not spoken or informal).
Summary: Reworded thesis statement — a review of the thesis and main points.
Clincher / Final thought: Inferred from your interpretation of the passages and your essay — a final overall lesson or connecting thought.

Text 1 — Informational Text (A1)
Communication in Shared Spaces
By Tevin Malcolm

A school club once relied on paper notices and announcements to tell members about schedule changes. Now its members use a group account. When a meeting room changes, a post may reach students before the school day ends. Yet a student who has turned off notifications might see the update only after arriving at the original room. Another student may hear about the change from a friend without opening the app at all.

Many families share ordinary moments in similar ways. A photograph from a school performance can reach a relative who lives far away. That relative may leave a comment, send a private message, or bring up the performance during a later call. A photograph can begin a conversation, though it may show only a small part of what happened that day.

Messages also move through communities. A resident might post that the library is collecting books. Neighbors can share the notice with others, but the date may be missing when someone copies only the picture. People who want to donate then have to find the original post or contact the library.

Readers make decisions with even shorter pieces of information in private conversations. A reply such as “Sure.” may seem friendly to one person and impatient to another. Punctuation, emojis, and the time a reply arrives can influence what a reader thinks the writer meant. Sometimes people ask a follow-up question; sometimes they carry their first impression into the next conversation.

A phone can also bring several exchanges into the same moment. During a family meal, someone may read a club update, answer a friend, and listen to a relative speaking across the table. The relative can see when the listener looks down, but the friend waiting for a reply cannot. Each person has a different view of the same few minutes.

Text 2 — Short Story
The Missing Page
By Stevan Johnson

By Thursday afternoon, the library’s display of student artwork was nearly ready. Leila had arranged the drawings by theme, and Noah was printing the small cards that named each artist. Then the printer stopped halfway through the last page.

Leila photographed the unfinished cards and sent the image to the club group. “Can someone finish these before tomorrow?” she wrote. Replies arrived while she was still clearing the table. One student offered a printer; another said she could bring blank cards. Noah, who had already left, noticed that the photograph did not show the names at the bottom of the page.

“Those are all the names?” he asked.

Leila read the message twice. She had spent an hour checking the list and thought Noah was questioning her work. She typed a reply, erased it, and put her phone in her bag. When she reached home, she found another message from him: “The photo cuts off the bottom. I have the full page here.”

The next morning, Noah brought the missing names. Leila told him why she had gone quiet, and he looked embarrassed. “I should have said what I meant,” he said. They finished the cards together before the first visitors arrived.

At lunch, a parent shared a picture of the display in a neighborhood group. Leila’s grandmother saw it and sent a message asking which drawing was hers. By the time Leila replied, her grandmother had already called Leila’s mother to ask. That evening, they talked about the drawing for much longer than Leila had expected.

On Friday, the club found one card beneath the printer. It belonged to a student whose drawing had been on display all day without a name. Leila placed the card beside it, then stepped back to check the whole wall.

He grinned and started from the top.
Outside, my brother called, “Come see!”
I sent one word to Grandma: Soon.
Then ran to find my family.

How TPFASTT Works

Tap each letter to see the full instructions.

T — Title
Read the title and make a prediction. What do you think this poem is about? You may also look at images included on the page, introductions, or footnotes to give you clues. It does not matter if your initial prediction is incorrect — you will revisit it later.
P — Paraphrase
What is happening in the poem? Read the poem and describe what is happening in your own words. Paraphrase the poem section-by-section. Do not summarize the whole poem in one sentence — instead, read through it slowly and paraphrase as you go.
F — Figurative
Authors use literary devices for a purpose. Identify as many literary devices as you can and consider why the author has included them, and what they mean. Record the line number, or annotate your poem directly. Look for figures of speech like similes, metaphors, personification, and so on, as well as poetic devices like rhyme scheme, rhythm, onomatopoeia, alliteration, etc. Also look at what FORM the poem is (i.e. sonnet, haiku, lyric, ballad, and so on).
A — Attitude
In other words, what is the tone of the poem? In order to find the tone, you must identify the subject of the poem (who or what the poem is about). List the words that the author uses to describe the subject. Are these words positive (softly, shine, sway, dance) or are these words negative (bare, torn, crack, knife)? Considering all of these clues — how does the author feel towards the subject? Choose a specific adjective (i.e. nostalgic, regretful, indifferent). Note that attitude may also shift throughout the poem.
S — Shift
Look for any changes in the poem. Does the mood change from one emotion to another? Does the tense change from present to past? Does the point-of-view change from first-person to second-person? Does the setting change? Does the speaker’s feelings or perspective change? Where does the shift happen (which line)? Why does this shift occur? If you are SURE there is no shift, say there is no shift in this poem.
T — Title (again)
Look at the title again. Was your prediction correct? Now that you have analyzed the poem — does the title mean anything new to you? Why do you think the author chose this title?
T — Theme
Theme = topic + insight. What topics are presented in the poem (i.e. culture, identity, love, revenge, etc.)? What is the poem telling us about the topic? What is the message that the author may be trying to convey to readers? Theme is not one word, and it is not a piece of advice like “Don’t do drugs.” It states something true to life, written as a full sentence.

Worked Example — “Spring”

This shows what a completed, high-quality analysis looks like, using a different poem. Use it as a model for depth and detail — do not copy its content into your own work.

See the completed example for “Spring” by Henry Gardiner Adams
A bursting into greenness;
A waking as from sleep;
A twitter and a warble
That make the pulses leap:
A watching, as in childhood,
For the flowers that, one by one,
Open their golden petals
To woo the fitful sun.
A gust, a flash, a gurgle,
A wish to shout and sing,
As, filled with hope and gladness,
We hail the vernal Spring.

by Henry Gardiner Adams

Title prediction: The title “Spring” likely refers to the season known for renewal, growth, and fresh beginnings.

Literal meaning, line by line

A bursting into greenness; — This line suggests the landscape is turning green as plants and trees sprout grow new leaves.
A waking as from sleep; — This personifies nature as awakening as if it had been asleep, likely a reference to the end of winter.
A twitter and a warble — The sounds of birds singing fill the air.
That make the pulses leap: — The sounds and sights of spring excite the listener, making their heartbeat faster.
A watching, as in childhood, — The poet observes nature with the same wonder as a child does.
For the flowers that, one by one, — Watching flowers bloom gradually.
Open their golden petals — The flowers reveal their yellow petals.
To woo the fitful sun. — The flowers seem to be enticing the sun, which is still uncertain and irregular in early spring.
A gust, a flash, a gurgle, — Describing the sounds and sights of a sudden breeze, a burst of light, and perhaps a stream or a brook.
A wish to shout and sing, — The speaker feels an urge to express joy vocally.
As, filled with hope and gladness, — The speaker feels hopeful and happy.
We hail the vernal Spring. — We welcome the spring season enthusiastically.

Stanza summary

The Poem only has one stanza: The first stanza describes the awakening of nature in spring, with the greening of plants and the joyful sounds of birds. It captures the excitement and freshness of spring as seen through the eyes of someone who is closely observing the natural changes. The poem continues with imagery of flowers blooming and interacting with the sun, capturing the dynamic and lively atmosphere of spring. The stanza concludes with an expression of joy and a celebratory welcome to the spring season.

Summary of events

The poem celebrates the arrival of spring, describing the renewal of nature with vivid imagery. It moves through various sensory experiences, including sights and sounds, to convey the joy and energy associated with this season of rebirth.

Understanding the poem

Who is the subject of the poem? The subject of the poem is nature in the spring season.
What are they talking about? The poem talks about the renewal and awakening of nature during spring, characterized by new growth, blooming flowers, and the joyful sounds of birds.
Why do you think the author wrote the poem? (Purpose) The author likely wrote the poem to celebrate the arrival of spring and to convey the joy, hope, and renewal associated with this season.
When is the poem happening? The poem is set during the early days of spring, as winter fades away and nature begins to bloom.
Where is the poem happening? The poem takes place outdoors, in a natural setting where one can observe the changing seasons.
What is the poet’s attitude? The poet’s attitude is one of joy, wonder, and appreciation for the beauty and renewal that spring brings.
How does the poem shift from person to person or between different times or places? The poem maintains a consistent perspective, focusing on the present moment of spring’s arrival. It shifts through different aspects of spring—from the greening of the landscape to the singing of birds to the blooming of flowers—but always remains centered on the theme of renewal and joy.

Poetic devices & figurative language

Literary Device: Alliteration
Quote: “A twitter and a warble”
Meaning: The repetition of the “w” sound in “twitter” and “warble” mimics the light, musical quality of birdsong, enhancing the joyful and lively mood of the poem.
Literary Device: Repetition
Quote: “A bursting into greenness; / A waking as from sleep; / A twitter and a warble /”
Meaning: The author repeats the word “A” at the beginning of several lines to create a rhythm that feels like the ongoing, repeating events of spring. This repetition makes the poem feel like it is building up, with each line adding to the feeling of spring arriving. It shows that spring is full of many different sights and sounds, making it feel like an exciting and lively time.
Figurative Language: Imagery
Quote: “Open their golden petals / To woo the fitful sun.”
Meaning: This vivid imagery creates a picture of flowers blooming and reaching out to the sun. The use of “woo” personifies the flowers, suggesting an almost romantic interaction with the sun, which adds depth to the portrayal of nature.
Figurative Language: Personification
Quote: “A waking as from sleep”
Meaning: This line personifies nature as if it were a person waking up after a long sleep. This emphasizes the idea of renewal and awakening that comes with spring.
Other Poetic Device: Symbolism
Quote: “To woo the fitful sun.”
Meaning: The “fitful sun” symbolizes the unpredictable weather often associated with early spring. It represents the transitional phase from winter to spring, where the sun’s warmth is still tentative.

Theme

The central theme of the poem is the renewal and joy that comes with the arrival of spring (the poem is about the happiness and new life that spring brings.) The poem shows hope, fresh starts, and the beauty of nature as it changes with the seasons. Using clear pictures and giving nature human-like qualities, the poem expresses the excitement and freshness of spring.

Title revisited

Looking at the title “Spring” again, it reinforces the central theme of the poem, which is the rejuvenation and renewal of life. Initially, the title might simply indicate the season, but after reading the poem, it becomes clear that “Spring” represents much more than just a time of year; it symbolizes a period of awakening, growth, and vibrant energy.
